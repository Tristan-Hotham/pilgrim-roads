I measured every waymarked pilgrim route I could find in OpenStreetMap, without counting any stretch twice: {{total.km|int}} km of them. More than half lead to Santiago de Compostela, and there is more of the Way of St James in Germany than in Spain.

In 1985 the Pilgrim's Office in Santiago de Compostela gave its certificate of completion, the Compostela, to {{compostela.first|int}} people. In 2025 it gave {{compostela.last|int}}. The revival of the Camino is one of the great travel stories of the last forty years, and it has not stayed in Spain. All over Europe, St James societies, hiking clubs and parishes have waymarked routes with the scallop shell, and volunteers have drawn many of them into OpenStreetMap, the free map of the world, tagged as pilgrimage routes. Alongside them sit the Via Francigena to Rome, the St Olav Ways to Trondheim and hundreds of smaller pilgrim paths.

I took {{source.fetched|int}} of those route relations, split them into tiny segments and counted each segment once, because the same path often belongs to a stage, a longer route and a cycling version at the same time. The naive total is {{source.km_raw|int}} km; without the double counting it is {{total.km|int}} km.

More Jakobsweg than Camino

{{fam.camino.km|int}} km of the routes are signed for Santiago, in {{fam.camino.n_countries|int}} countries. Spain has {{fam.camino.by_iso.ESP|int}} km of them. Germany has {{fam.camino.by_iso.DEU|int}} km of Jakobswege, {{fam.camino.de_es_ratio|f1}} times as much, and Poland {{fam.camino.by_iso.POL|int}} km of Drogi św. Jakuba. Bavaria's network alone runs to {{longest.1.km|int}} km.

{{IMG|b_camino.png|Horizontal bar chart of waymarked Camino de Santiago kilometres by country: Germany 14,814 km in red at the top, Spain 9,922 km in blue, Poland 5,289 km, then Austria, Croatia, Czechia, Italy, Denmark, Portugal, Slovenia, Slovakia, Hungary, Switzerland and France with 462 km|Waymarked Camino de Santiago in OpenStreetMap, by country.}}

Two cautions. This measures what is waymarked and mapped, not where people walk: in 2024, {{wiki.sarria_pct_2024|int}}% of certificate holders started at Sarria, on the last stretch of the Camino Francés. And France, with only {{fam.camino.fr_km|int}} km, is badly undercounted. Several of its great routes, the Via Turonensis and the Via Tolosana among them, are missing from my download, and others are probably mapped as hiking trails without a pilgrimage tag.

All roads lead to Santiago, eventually

Measure each kilometre of Camino by its straight line distance from the cathedral and the shape of the network appears. Only {{fam.camino.within500_pct|int}}% lies within 500 km of Santiago. Half of it is more than {{fam.camino.median_d|int}} km away, and the busiest band, {{fam.camino.max_band|raw}}, holds {{fam.camino.max_band_pct|int}}%, mostly in Germany, Austria and Croatia. The farthest waymarked stretch is the {{fam.camino.far_route|raw}} in {{fam.camino.far_country|raw}}, {{fam.camino.far_km|int}} km from the tomb of St James as the crow flies.

To Rome, to Trondheim, to Mary

The rest of the map falls into a few families. The Via Francigena, the medieval road from Canterbury to Rome that Archbishop Sigeric described around {{wiki.vf_year|int}}, has {{fam.francigena.km|int}} km, {{fam.francigena.it_pct|int}}% of it in Italy, and with its variants and its extension south of Rome it is the longest single system in the data, {{longest.0.km|int}} km. The St Olav Ways to Nidaros Cathedral in Trondheim have {{fam.olav.km|int}} km in Norway and Sweden. The Marian ways, mostly the Via Mariae network centred on Hungary, have {{fam.marian.km|int}} km. Everything else, from Germany's Lutherweg to the roads to Assisi, adds {{fam.other.km|int}} km.

Where pilgrims go

Wikidata lists {{sites.n|int}} pilgrimage sites in {{sites.n_countries|int}} countries. Germany has the most ({{sites.by_iso.DEU|int}}, mostly pilgrimage churches), then Italy ({{sites.by_iso.ITA|int}}), Spain ({{sites.by_iso.ESP|int}}) and Japan ({{sites.by_iso.JPN|int}}), where {{sites.shikoku|int}} sit on Shikoku, the island of the {{wiki.shikoku_temples|int}} temple pilgrimage. Per head, Austria leads with {{sites.dens.0.per_m|f1}} per million people. Where Wikidata records a religion, {{sites.rec_christian_pct|int}}% of sites are Christian, but it records one for only {{sites.rec_pct|int}}% of them.

How many walk

The certificate counts tell the story of the revival: {{compostela.y1993|int}} in the Holy Year of 1993, {{compostela.y2010|int}} in 2010, a pandemic collapse to {{compostela.y2020|int}} in 2020 and a record {{compostela.last|int}} in 2025. What the map leaves out

This is a map of what volunteers have drawn, and they have mostly drawn Europe: {{total.europe_pct|f1}}% of the route kilometres. The biggest pilgrimages on Earth are missing entirely. The Hajj drew {{wiki.hajj_2026|int}} pilgrims to Mecca in 2026, the Arba'een walk to Karbala draws {{wiki.arbaeen|raw}} people a year, and about {{wiki.kumbh_2025_m|int}} million attended the Kumbh Mela in 2025, yet none appears as a mapped route. Even in Europe the download is incomplete: {{source.ids_failed|int}} of {{source.ids_requested|int}} relation ids failed, and the Camino Francés into Santiago itself is among the missing pieces. Every total here is a lower bound.

How I did this

The routes are OpenStreetMap relations tagged as pilgrimage routes, downloaded through the Overpass API. I left out a road, a bus route, a ski piste and a handful of trails tagged by mistake, split every way into point to point segments with coordinates to about 10 m, and counted each segment once. Families come from simple rules on route names in many languages, Wikidata links and the scallop shell symbol; stages inherit the family of their route. Countries come from Natural Earth outlines, distances from Santiago are straight lines, and the sites come from Wikidata. The certificate counts and historical facts come from Wikipedia. The paper sets out every step and caveat.
