Pilgrim roads

Every waymarked pilgrim route I could find in OpenStreetMap, {{total.km|int}} km of path, and {{sites.n|int}} pilgrimage sites from Wikidata. The giant is the Way of St James: {{fam.camino.km|int}} km signed for Santiago de Compostela, spread over {{fam.camino.n_countries|int}} countries, with more of it in Germany than in Spain.

Early in the ninth century, tradition holds, the remains of the apostle James were found in Galicia, and roads began to form towards his tomb. After 1492 Pope Alexander VI declared the Camino de Santiago one of the three great pilgrimages of Christendom, with Jerusalem and the Via Francigena to Rome. Five centuries later it was a quiet road: in 1985 the Pilgrim's Office in Santiago gave its certificate, the Compostela, to {{compostela.first|int}} people. In 2025 it gave {{compostela.last|int}}.

The revival was not only Spanish. Across Europe, St James societies, hiking clubs and parishes have waymarked old and new routes with the scallop shell, until the Camino became a web reaching from Portugal to Ukraine. Volunteers have mapped much of it in OpenStreetMap, alongside the Via Francigena to Rome, the St Olav Ways to Trondheim, the Marian ways of central Europe and hundreds of smaller pilgrim paths. This page puts all of them on one map, measures them without counting any stretch twice, and adds the shrines and temples that Wikidata records as places of pilgrimage. It also says plainly what is missing, which is a lot of the world.

    02 · The Camino webMore Jakobsweg than Camino

    Most people picture the Camino as one road across northern Spain, the Camino Francés, about {{wiki.frances_km|int}} km from Roncesvalles to Santiago. In 2024 nearly half of all certificate holders, {{wiki.frances_pct_2024|int}}%, walked it, and {{wiki.sarria_pct_2024|int}}% started in Sarria, just over the {{wiki.min_walk_km|int}} km minimum walk for a Compostela. But the routes signed for Santiago are far longer than that. In OpenStreetMap I count {{fam.camino.km|int}} km of them in {{fam.camino.n_countries|int}} countries, and Spain holds only {{fam.camino.es_pct|int}}%.

    Waymarked Camino de Santiago, by country

    Unique kilometres of routes signed for Santiago, whatever the local name: Camino, Caminho, Jakobsweg, Droga św. Jakuba, Svatojakubská cesta, Jakobova pot. Hover a bar; click to fly there.

    Germany has the most: {{fam.camino.by_iso.DEU|int}} km of Jakobswege, {{fam.camino.de_es_ratio|f2}} times Spain's {{fam.camino.by_iso.ESP|int}} km. Poland follows with {{fam.camino.by_iso.POL|int}} km of Drogi św. Jakuba, then Austria ({{fam.camino.by_iso.AUT|int}} km), Croatia ({{fam.camino.by_iso.HRV|int}} km) and Czechia ({{fam.camino.by_iso.CZE|int}} km). Bavaria alone carries a network of {{longest.1.km|int}} km. Two cautions. First, this measures what is waymarked and mapped, not where pilgrims walk: most Compostela holders walk only the last stretch in Spain, and in 2024 nearly a third started at Sarria. Second, France looks almost empty, with {{fam.camino.fr_km|int}} km. Several of the great French routes, the Via Turonensis and the Via Tolosana among them, are relations missing from this download (section 07), and others are probably mapped as long distance hiking trails without a pilgrimage tag. The real French figure is much larger.

    There is {{fam.camino.de_es_ratio|f1}} times as much waymarked Way of St James in Germany as in Spain.

    03 · Fanning outAll roads lead to Santiago, eventually

    Measure every kilometre of Camino by its straight line distance from the cathedral in Santiago and the shape of the network appears. Only {{fam.camino.within500_pct|int}}% lies within 500 km. Half of it is more than {{fam.camino.median_d|int}} km away, and the busiest band, {{fam.camino.max_band|raw}}, holds {{fam.camino.max_band_pct|int}}% of the total, most of it in Germany, Austria and Croatia. {{fam.camino.over1000_pct|int}}% is more than 1,000 km from the tomb of St James. Turn on the distance rings in the map to see it.

    04 · Other roadsTo Rome, to Trondheim, to Mary

    The Camino is just over half of all the pilgrim routes mapped. The rest fall into a few families.

    Waymarked pilgrim routes by family

    Unique kilometres. A stretch used by two families counts in both ({{total.multi_family_km|int}} km do), so the bars add up to a little more than the {{total.km|int}} km total. Hover a bar.

    The Via Francigena ({{fam.francigena.km|int}} km) is the medieval road to Rome from Canterbury, documented by Archbishop Sigeric around {{wiki.vf_year|int}} in {{wiki.vf_stages|int}} stages, some {{wiki.vf_sigeric_km|raw}} km. In OpenStreetMap it is mapped as one system of {{longest.0.km|int}} km, the longest in the data, because its variants, its extension south of Rome towards Apulia and Sicily's own Francigene count too. {{fam.francigena.it_pct|int}}% of it is in Italy.

    The St Olav Ways ({{fam.olav.km|int}} km) lead to Nidaros Cathedral in Trondheim, the tomb of Norway's saint king. Wikipedia gives the main route from Oslo as about {{wiki.olav_km|int}} km; the mapped network adds the Swedish St Olavsleden from the Baltic coast and the Østerdalen, Borg and Nordleden paths. {{fam.olav.no_pct|int}}% is in Norway.

    The Marian ways ({{fam.marian.km|int}} km) lead to shrines of the Virgin Mary. Most belong to the Mária Út, or Via Mariae, a network that runs from Austria through Hungary into Romania, Slovakia, Croatia and Bosnia: Hungary alone has {{fam.marian.by_iso.HUN|int}} km. Portugal's Caminhos de Fátima add {{fam.marian.by_iso.PRT|int}} km.

    Everything else ({{fam.other.km|int}} km) ranges from the Lutherweg in Germany and the roads to Assisi in Italy to Sweden's pilgrim paths, Britain's revived saints' ways and, far from Europe, the Kumano Kodō in Japan and the Caminho da Graça to Aparecida in Brazil.

    The longest mapped pilgrim route systems

    Top-level OpenStreetMap relations with all their stages, branches and variants, each stretch counted once. "Tag" is the length the mappers wrote in the relation's distance tag, where there is one. Click a row to see it on the map.

    Systems with many branches win that table. Counting single relations instead, with no child routes, the longest is the {{longest_leaf.0.name|raw}} in {{longest_leaf.0.country|raw}}, {{longest_leaf.0.km|int}} km, followed by the {{longest_leaf.1.name|raw}} in {{longest_leaf.1.country|raw}} ({{longest_leaf.1.km|int}} km) and the {{longest_leaf.2.name|raw}} ({{longest_leaf.2.km|int}} km). Half of all single relations are shorter than {{leaf_median_km|int}} km: most mappers record a pilgrim road one stage at a time.

    05 · Where pilgrims go{{sites.n|int}} places of pilgrimage

    Wikidata lists {{sites.n|int}} places it classes as pilgrimage sites, in {{sites.n_countries|int}} countries. Germany has the most ({{sites.by_iso.DEU|int}}, mostly Wallfahrtskirchen, pilgrimage churches), then Italy ({{sites.by_iso.ITA|int}}), Spain ({{sites.by_iso.ESP|int}}) and Japan ({{sites.by_iso.JPN|int}}). Japan's are almost all on one island: {{sites.shikoku|int}} sit on Shikoku, home of the {{wiki.shikoku_temples|int}} temple pilgrimage of the monk Kūkai, a walking circuit of about {{wiki.shikoku_km|raw}} km. Relative to population, Austria leads, with {{sites.dens.0.per_m|f1}} sites per million people, ahead of Portugal and Czechia.

    Pilgrimage sites by country

    Wikidata items classed as pilgrimage sites. Switch between the count and the rate per million people (countries with 10 or more sites). Hover a bar; click to fly there.

    Wikidata records a religion for only {{sites.rec_n|int}} of the sites ({{sites.rec_pct|int}}%), and {{sites.rec_christian_pct|int}}% of those are Christian, almost all Catholic. Reading the rest by their names (a "Wallfahrtskirche", a "Santuário", a "-ji Temple") gives {{sites.trad.Christian|int}} Christian sites, {{sites.trad.Buddhist|int}} Buddhist, {{sites.trad.Muslim|int}} Muslim and {{sites.trad.Hindu|int}} Hindu, with {{sites.trad.Not recorded|int}} still unknown. That split says more about who edits Wikidata, and in which languages, than about where the world's pilgrims go.

    Do the mapped roads lead to the listed shrines? Only partly. {{sites.near5|int}} sites ({{sites.near5_pct|int}}%) lie within 5 km of a mapped pilgrim route, {{sites.eu_near5_pct|int}}% of those in Europe. Most shrines in Wikidata are local goals, not the ends of long waymarked roads, and many of those roads lead to a single great goal such as Santiago, Rome or Trondheim.

    06 · How many walkFrom {{compostela.first|int}} to {{compostela.last|int}}

    The Pilgrim's Office counts the certificates it issues to those who walk at least the last {{wiki.min_walk_km|int}} km, or cycle {{wiki.min_cycle_km|int}} km. The numbers, as Wikipedia tabulates them, show the revival: {{compostela.first|int}} in 1985, {{compostela.y1993|int}} in the Holy Year of 1993, {{compostela.y2010|int}} in 2010, a collapse to {{compostela.y2020|int}} in the first year of the pandemic, and a record {{compostela.last|int}} in 2025, {{compostela.x_since_1985|int}} times the 1985 figure. Holy Years, when St James's day on 25 July falls on a Sunday, bring spikes; the next is {{wiki.holy_year_next|int}}.

    Compare that with the biggest pilgrimages on Earth. The Hajj to Mecca drew {{wiki.hajj_2026|int}} pilgrims in 2026; the Arba'een walk to Karbala in Iraq draws {{wiki.arbaeen|raw}} people a year, many on foot from Najaf; and about {{wiki.kumbh_2025_m|int}} million attended the Kumbh Mela in India in 2025. Not one of those journeys appears on the map above.

    07 · What the map leaves outA European picture of a world habit

    This is a map of what volunteers have chosen to draw and tag, and they have mostly drawn Europe. {{total.europe_pct|f1}}% of the route kilometres are in Europe. Outside it there are just {{total.outside_km|int}} km in {{total.n_outside_countries|int}} countries: the Caminho da Graça to Aparecida in Brazil ({{total.outside.0.km|int}} km), {{total.outside.1.km|int}} km in Japan, mostly the Kumano Kodō, and a few short paths in Israel, India and elsewhere in Asia. The Shikoku circuit, {{wiki.shikoku_km|raw}} km, is not in the route data at all, only its temples. Wikidata knows the Darb Zubayda, the {{wd.darb_zubayda_km|int}} km Abbasid pilgrim road from Kufa to Mecca, but no OpenStreetMap route draws it. The sites are less lopsided but still {{sites.europe_pct|int}}% European.

    Gaps in the download

    The route list came from {{source.ids_requested|int}} OpenStreetMap relation ids. {{source.failed_batches|int}} batches of {{source.ids_failed|int}} ids in all ({{source.failed_pct|f1}}%) failed to download and are missing. Container relations in the data list {{source.subrels_missing|int}} child relations that are not in the extract, and {{source.containers_all_missing|int}} containers have none of their children. Of the {{wd.with_osm|int}} Wikidata pilgrim route items (stations of the cross aside) that name an OpenStreetMap relation, {{wd.with_osm_missing|int}} are missing, among them the Camino Francés as a whole, the Via Turonensis, the Via Tolosana, the Sanabrés and Catalan ways and the Romboleden to Trondheim. Some Spanish stages are here, but the final stretch of the Camino Francés into Santiago is not. Every figure on this page is therefore a lower bound for what OpenStreetMap holds.

    08 · How I did thisMethods and caveats

    Routes. {{source.fetched|int}} OpenStreetMap route relations tagged as pilgrimage routes (or linked to Wikidata pilgrim route items), downloaded from the Overpass API in {{source.osm_year|raw}}. I kept {{source.kept|int}}, leaving out {{source.dropped|int}}: a road, a bus route, a ski piste, a fitness trail, network relations, and six that inspection showed are not pilgrimage routes (a Michigan forest trail named after the Pilgrim River, for instance). {{source.containers|int}} kept relations are containers for others, mostly super-routes made of stages.

    No double counting. The same path often belongs to several relations: a stage and its super-route, two routes that share a valley, a cycling and a walking version. I split every way into its point to point segments, with coordinates to four decimal places (about 10 m), and counted each segment once whichever relation and direction it came from. The raw sum of all relations' ways is {{source.km_raw|int}} km; without duplicates it is {{source.km|int}} km, {{source.dup_pct|f1}}% less. Each segment's country is the Natural Earth country at its midpoint.

    Families. A relation is Camino if its names (in any language), tags or Wikidata link point to St James or Santiago, or if it carries the scallop shell symbol ({{fam.camino.shell_rel|int}} relations were placed by the shell alone); Via Francigena, St Olav and Marian ways by similar rules; anything else is "other". Stages without a telling name take the family of the route they belong to. The rules are in the code and every relation's family, and the reason for it, is in the data file.

    Sites. {{sites.rows|int}} rows from a Wikidata query for pilgrimage sites, {{sites.n|int}} distinct items, {{sites.unlabelled|int}} without an English label. Country from Wikidata, checked against the coordinates. Tradition from Wikidata's religion where given; the name based reading is a rough guide only.

    What this is not. A count of pilgrims, or of the routes people actually use. OpenStreetMap is uneven: some countries' volunteers map every waymarked path, others map few, and tagging habits differ (France's long distance trails, for one). Straight line distance from Santiago is not walking distance. Wikipedia supplies the historical facts and the certificate counts.
